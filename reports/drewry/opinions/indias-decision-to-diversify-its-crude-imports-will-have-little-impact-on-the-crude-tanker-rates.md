# India’s decision to diversify its crude imports will have little impact on the crude tanker rates

*01 Apr 2021*

India’s recent decision to diversify crude imports to reduce the dependence on Middle Eastern crude will have little impact on the freight rates in the crude tanker market. Although an increase in long-haul crude imports by India from the Atlantic basin will boost tonne-mile demand for crude tankers in the short term,  ongoing oversupply in the market will cap any surge in freight rates.

Crude oil prices have been on a mainly upward trend since November 2020 on the back of a gradual recovery in oil demand and production curbs by OPEC+ members. With reforms in fuel taxation and the subsidy system, consumers in India are now more directly exposed to crude oil price changes. Hit hard by rising crude oil prices in recent months, India is worried that high fuel prices will derail its recovery from the economic hit arising from COVID-19.

New Delhi had urged OPEC producers to ease output cuts to facilitate the global economic recovery. However, the OPEC+ producers, led by Saudi Arabia, decided to extend the output curbs into April 2021 and in response to India’s request to increase output, Riyadh asked New Delhi to use oil India bought cheaply during the price collapse in 2020. Annoyed by the extension of the production cut by OPEC+, the Indian government has asked domestic refiners to aggressively look for diversification to reduce dependence on Middle Eastern producers.

India is the world's third-largest crude oil importer, and 63% of its crude oil comes from the Middle East. As Indian refineries are geared to process mainly medium to heavy, high-sulphur crude, finding alternatives to Middle Eastern crude in the current market conditions when the availability of heavy crude is tight will be difficult.

The availability of heavy crude has tightened because of US sanctions on Venezuela and Iran. In the absence of heavy crude from Venezuela and Iran, the only available alternatives to Middle Eastern crude are heavy crude from Canada and Mexico and medium sweet crude from Brazil and Guyana. There is minimal scope for an increase in crude production and exports from Mexico and the chances for Canadian crude moving beyond the US are hampered by limited pipeline capacity. Although crude exports from Brazil and Guyana will increase in the future, India will have to compete with China and the US for medium crude supply from these South American countries.

As the majority of the global refinery capacity expansion over the next five years will be in China and India, the eastward flow of crude from all the sources will increase. However, any increase in India’s share in heavy crude exports from the Americas at the expense of China will not significantly change tonne-mile demand for crude tankers.

This is due to the fact that an any increase in tonne-mile demand arising from India replacing short-haul Middle Eastern crude with long-haul crude from the Americas will be counterbalanced by a decline in tonne-mile demand on Chinese imports, as the latter will be forced to increase its short-haul imports from the Middle East. That said, a possible return of Venezuelan crude would certainly boost tonne-mile demand for crude tankers as it would lead to a surge in India’s long-haul crude imports from this South American exporter.

Meanwhile, the decision by the Indian government to reduce dependence on Middle Eastern crude will have a positive impact on the tonne-mile demand for crude tankers in the short term. There is plenty of light crude available from West Africa, North Sea and the US because of the weakness in refinery runs in Europe. Although India’s complex refineries are geared to process heavy sour crude, the narrow price premium of light sweet crudes relative to the heavy sour grades has resulted in an increase in the share of light crude in India’s imports over the last year.

Moreover, demand for lighter feedstock from Indian refiners has surged in recent months with the speedy recovery in domestic demand for lighter products such as gasoline, naphtha, and LPG to pre-pandemic levels. On the other hand, demand for middle distillates such as diesel and jet fuel are still significantly lower than pre-pandemic levels. With a shift in consumption towards lighter barrels, crude inflows from the US to India surged past inflows from Saudi Arabia in February. As the demand for middle distillate will take longer to recover, we can expect further increases in Atlantic basin light crude flows to India in the short term, which will boost tonne-mile demand for crude tankers. However, overcapacity in the tanker market will cap any surge in rates in the short-term.
