# ‘The Great Indian Lockdown’ pushes suppliers to cut production and shut plants

*30 Apr 2020*

India imposed the nationwide lockdown on 24 March 2020 and extended it to 3 May 2020 to stem the spread of coronavirus (COVID-19) in the country. The country is also considering a 15% COVID-19 tax from 1 May 2020 to 31 March 2021 on imports of some chemicals (list is unclear) to protect its domestic industry which is suffering from slowdown in economic activity, due to the lockdown and collapsed oil prices. As a result, we project a 40% drop in domestic demand for chemicals/vegoils in 2Q20 year on year which will translate into a fall in demand of 2.6 million tonnes in 2020 (over 2019). While many suppliers are already cutting production and even shutting down plants in anticipation of the weak demand from India, any extension of the lockdown by a few more months will have severe implications for the chemical shipping sector.

Vegoil imports are expected to decline sharply in 2Q20 due to lower consumption during the lockdown despite Ramadan when demand is usually high. Stocking for this peak in demand starts in the beginning of April in anticipation of increased sales in the entire 40-day period, but this year has been very different.

The massive decline in palm oil imports to India (one of the largest consumers of palm oil) in 2Q20 can be attributed to the fall in demand from restaurants and hotels which account for more than two-thirds of the total palm oil consumption, reducing one million tonnes of trade on the Straits to India. Vegoil freight rates on this route will be under pressure in 2Q20 not only due to the weak demand but also limited palm oil exports from Malaysia and Indonesia following lockdowns in both exporting countries.

The demand for sunflower oil and soybean oil will not observe such a drastic decline as they are used in home kitchens. The narrowing price gap of $14-38 per tonne in 1Q20 (from $57-152 per tonne in 4Q19) between palm oil and soybean oil will support the gradual switch from palm oil to other vegoils in hotels and restaurants, lowering the demand for palm oil. Meanwhile, supply chains for sunflower oil and soybean oil will remain more or less stable on long-haul routes as India imports soybean oil from South America and sunflower oil from the Black Sea region.

Methanol trade collapsed in April 2020 due to low downstream demand from the formaldehyde sector which accounts for three-fourths of the total methanol consumption in India. This sector requires huge manpower, which is usually migrant labour from rural areas. Since these migrant workers have already moved to their hometowns after the lockdown, resuming production will become increasingly challenging when the lockdown ends. Massive reduction in manpower as well as transportation and storage issues will further worsen downstream demand and trade.

India imports on average 193,600 tonnes of methanol every month. However, the lockdown has weakened demand and made the discharge of methanol cargoes difficult due to logistical constraints leading to high methanol stocks in the ports of Kandla, Mundra and Mumbai. As a result, the Middle East is redirecting methanol cargoes to China and Southeast Asia which is pushing up the supplies in these regions. Since India is among the largest consumers of methanol and sources it mainly from the Middle East, many suppliers of the Middle East will be forced to cut production or even shut down their plants due to decreased demand from India. However, Iranian methanol producers are the worst hit by the lockdown in India as Iran has limited options to divert cargos with US sanctions still in place.

The lockdown in India has also adversely impacted styrene imports. The polymer sector, which is the major consumer of styrene, requires huge manpower for packaging and manufacturing plastic materials and goods. As the migrant workers have already moved to their hometowns, production of plastic materials and goods will become difficult when the lockdown ends. Moreover, plants have high inventories which could make it even more challenging for the recovery in shipping demand in 2020.

A demand-pull after the lockdown ends seems highly unlikely as projected GDP growth for 2020 is negative. Industries that consume styrene (automobiles, buildings and construction) are the worst hit by the pandemic. While a few industries such as packaging and medical devices can lift the demand in late 2020, when economic activity resumes, demand will still be lower than in 2019.

India imported 0.9 million tonnes of styrene in 2019 with the US contributing 28.3% of India’s total imports followed by South Korea (19.5%) and Singapore (16.0%). However, styrene supply from the US will be lower in 2Q20 as the planned turnaround of INEOS Styrolution’s 455,000 tonnes per year plant was planned to be completed by the end of April. However, due to weakening demand, the plant will not restart its operations anytime soon.

We are optimistic that COVID-19 will be contained in 2H20 and the demand for chemicals will revive by end-2020. India will come out of the shadow of lockdown into the sunshine with vessels reaching its shores and discharging their cargoes.
