# From coal to critical minerals: How the energy transition is reshaping dry bulk trade

*27 Jan 2026*

Dry bulk shipping is entering another phase of structural evolution. While coal’s long-term decline is inevitable, the material intensity of electrification and deployment of renewable energy are creating new trade flows, particularly in lithium-bearing ores such as spodumene and lepidolite. These cargoes, shipped as dry bulk, are scaling up fast enough to support vessel employment, utilisation and charter rate stability, especially in the Handysize and Supramax segments. In this sense, the energy transition is not eliminating dry bulk demand, but is redefining it.

Dry bulk shipping is being reshaped by the global energy transition. While traditional cargoes such as coal, iron ore and grain have historically dominated trade, new material-intensive commodities are increasingly entering seaborne flows, supporting vessel utilisation and charter rates. For example, the surge in global electric vehicle (EV) sales has been driving a corresponding rise in lithium-ion battery demand over the past decade. These shifts are creating a material impact on dry bulk flows, particularly for small- and mid-sized vessels tasked with moving raw materials that feed this transition.

Among these emerging commodities, lithium ores—spodumene and lepidolite—stand out for both scale and strategic importance. Mined and shipped as bulk concentrates, they form the primary source of lithium, a key component in EV batteries and stationary energy storage systems. Their bulk nature makes them natural candidates for dry bulk transport, generating long-haul tonne-mile demand and supporting vessel employment across Handysize, Supramax and Panamax segments.

Spodumene has emerged as the dominant lithium ore in seaborne trade. Global trade grew from around 3.0 million tonnes in 2015 to over 4.0 million tonnes by 2021–22, dipped slightly in 2023 due to inventory adjustments, and recovered to 5.3 million tonnes in 2024 and an estimated 6.8 million tonnes in 2025. Lepidolite, while smaller in scale, is increasingly mined as an alternative source.

From a shipping perspective, spodumene is particularly relevant for small- and mid-sized vessels. Parcel sizes and port constraints favour Handysize and Supramax vessels, while Panamaxes are deployed on long-haul trades from Australia, Brazil and Africa to China and the US. This contributes to backhaul employment, higher utilisation and incremental support for charter rates. Importantly, demand extends beyond passenger EVs: growth in electric buses, trucks and grid-scale storage systems increases battery capacity per unit, sustaining lithium ore shipments even if EV sales moderate in some economies such as China.

Lepidolite, while smaller in scale, is gaining importance as an alternative lithium source, particularly where spodumene deposits are limited or harder to develop. India has emerged as a major exporter of lepidolite, largely to the US, while Brazil, Pakistan and Australia are also expanding production.

Southern Africa represents both opportunity and uncertainty. Namibia and Zimbabwe have significant lepidolite potential, but policy interventions are shaping trade flows. Zimbabwe’s ban on raw lithium ore exports until at least 2027 aims to promote domestic refining, likely reducing seaborne exports of unprocessed lepidolite while increasing future shipments of concentrates or chemicals. Meanwhile, on the import side, China remains the dominant destination for both spodumene and lepidolite, reflecting its unmatched battery manufacturing and refining capacity.

The underlying driver of this trade expansion is the rapid growth in electric mobility. Global EV sales have risen sharply over the past decade, supported by policy mandates, subsidies and falling battery costs. Battery demand, measured in gigawatt hours, has grown even faster, reflecting not only higher sales volumes but also rising battery sizes per vehicle.

Importantly for dry bulk shipping, lithium demand is not limited to passenger cars. Stationary energy storage systems, which support renewable power grids by balancing intermittency, are becoming a major growth segment. These systems are highly material-intensive and rely on the same lithium supply chains as EVs. As renewable penetration increases, so does the need for storage, reinforcing long-term lithium demand even if EV sales growth moderates in some regions.

China’s EV market illustrates this dynamic. While total vehicle sales may plateau as the passenger car and two-wheeler segments mature, policy emphasis is shifting towards electric buses, trucks and commercial fleets. These vehicles require significantly larger battery packs, meaning the demand for total battery capacity, and by extension lithium consumption, continues to rise even if unit sales growth slows. For dry bulk markets, this distinction matters more than headline EV sales numbers.

Lithium ores are transitioning from niche cargoes to structurally relevant components of dry bulk trade. Spodumene and lepidolite are generating new, long-haul shipping demand centred on China and the US, with supply increasingly diversified across Australia, Latin America, Africa and South Asia. The growth in lithium ore shipments offers a clear, near-to-medium-term source of support for small- and mid-sized dry bulk vessels as the energy transition reshapes global trade flows.
