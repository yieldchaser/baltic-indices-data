# Australian LNG strikes create anxiety in LNG market and shipping

*23 Aug 2023*

Possible labour strikes at the three major Australian LNG terminals have the potential to disrupt nearly 10% of the global LNG supply. The stoppages could tighten LNG supply in the market, especially amid the winter restocking period, leading to spikes in spot prices. Meanwhile, shipping rates could plunge as the majority of LNGCs associated with the projects become idle.

The unresolved disputes over pay and working conditions at Australian gas facilities owned and operated by Woodside Energy and Chevron have instigated the labour union officials to opt for an industrial strike, disrupting operations at three Australian gas platforms - North West Shelf (16.3 mtpa), Wheatstone (15.6 mtpa) and Gorgon (8.9 mtpa) – which account for 10% of the global LNG supply.

At present, the unions are voting on whether to initiate the strikes, which is expected to be decided by 24 August.

The long-running discussions between the energy firms and labour union officials have created havoc in the LNG market, destabilising spot prices.

Over 90% of Australian LNG exports move to Asia; however, the looming threat of potential strikes at key Australian LNG terminals triggered fear of supply disruptions as far as Europe, with the region reacting more aggressively than Asia – bringing TTF at a premium over Asian Spot. Even though the region’s storage is full at 90% as of 10 August, this spike in TTF indicates Europe’s risk of exposure to any disruption in LNG supply, which has been attributed to the region’s increased dependence on the LNG spot market since the start of the Russia-Ukraine war.

Meanwhile, limited contracted supply and the region’s long-term ambition to be net zero by 2050 have made spot purchases a last resort for Europe to avert the energy crisis.

Most LNG shipments from Australia head to Japan, China and South Korea, accounting for 36%, 26% and 18% of total Australian LNG exports during 1H23, respectively. Thus, a fall in shipments from Australia in case of a halt in operations at three LNG export terminals, which command about 46% of the total liquefaction capacity of the country, will increase competition for LNG, compelling Asian buyers to secure LNG cargoes, especially with demand in major importing countries (Japan, China, South Korea) starts to accelerate ahead of winter.

Although the impact would be limited in the short run with some volatility in prices, the squeezed supply from Australia will tighten the existing supply conditions, especially towards winter, when both Europe and Asia would need to meet the increased winter demand due to the evolved El Nino which is expected to take the temperature lower. Tight supply conditions and increased competition between Europe and Asia will bring in price volatility, crowding out price-sensitive countries in Asia (India, Thailand, Bangladesh) again, which began importing amid easing spot prices in 1H23.

Shipping rates, which are currently hovering around $100,000pd for a TFDE LNG carrier, are likely to strengthen further with the approaching winter season. However, if the Australian unions fail to reach a conclusion and do not force back their decision, the resulting strikes (and their duration) in the coming month will disrupt around 10% of the global LNG supply. The lost supply will impact shipping demand as it would prove to be difficult to source replacement cargoes during tight market conditions.

We believe the outages could lead to the idling of the majority of LNGCs associated with Australian projects. At the same time, some would be reprogrammed to transport LNG to Asia from farther locations. On average, 30 LNGCs per month will be inactive if strikes materialise, increasing the number of open vessels, which will exert downward pressure on shipping rates, especially during the peak season when rates are usually at their annual highs.

Meanwhile, increased Asian demand for spot cargoes from alternate suppliers will entail longer voyages, boosting tonne-mile demand, which will somewhat support the shipping rates. However, the complete substitution of reduced shipments from Australia is highly unlikely, evading any significant uptick in charter rates. Since the duration of the strikes remains uncertain, any prolongation of these strikes could lead to more drastic scenarios in the long run.

Additionally, the limited capacity addition and the deterioration of supplies at existing export facilities will also tighten the overall LNG supply ahead of winter. Russian supply to Asia will become more prominent in case the situation in Australia prolongs as the former is closet to Asia compared to other exporters. A significant fall in Australian exports coupled with the upcoming winter demand will stoke competition between Europe and Asia, entailing spikes in gas prices while incentivising exporters from the US and Middle East to increase their shipments.

Tight supply will likely persist until 2025, with 92 mtpa of liquefaction capacity due to come online. Thus, any disruption in the existing supply will prompt volatility in the LNG market, introducing new price shocks.

The strike in Australia is not the only example demonstrating the gas price volatility, which has become a new normal amid the Russia-Ukraine war; the supply outage at Norway’s Hammerfest LNG terminal in May was also a piece of evidence indicating the proneness of the gas prices to any supply disruptions.
