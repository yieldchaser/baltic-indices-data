# Trump’s return stokes positivity in LNG shipping, but not all is roses

*29 Nov 2024*

Trump’s re-election as US president is viewed as positive news for the LNG industry. His pro-LNG approach is expected to lift the ongoing barriers on US LNG export permits and licensing imposed by the Biden administration, which slowed down the pace of FIDs as well as the US supply deals. Drewry holds a similar stance but expects that the progress will be gradual, with considerable momentum in the LNG market likely toward 2H25. Meanwhile, growing speculation about the US-China trade war and geopolitics can hurt LNG shipping and project development.

The Biden administration’s pause on LNG export permits and licenses in January this year slowed LNG FID approvals and supply deals. None of the planned projects, including Delfin FLNG, Corpus Christi Phase 3 Midscale (Block 8-9), Driftwood LNG and Freeport LNG T4, were able to reach their FIDs in 2024 despite securing their DOE and FERC approvals.

According toDrewry’s FID watch, the 100 mtpa of pre-FID capacity, which was earlier expected to secure FID in 2024, has shifted to subsequent years, with more than 50% of pre-FID volumes in the US. Moreover, the ongoing deceleration in the FID pace has pushed deadlines for several projects up to 2030 and cancelled some planned capacity.

Furthermore, US LNG supply deals also slowed down in 2024 compared to last year; only 5.2 mtpa of sale and purchase agreements (SPAs) have been signed so far this year (as of end-November 2024), compared to 21.2 mtpa in 2023.

The slowdown in the US LNG market invoked the bearish market sentiment, clouded by uncertainty over the future of LNG. However, the conclusion of the 2024 US Presidential Election with Trump’s return appears to restore some positive sentiments in the LNG market. The FID pace will likely pick up from the following year, with more LNG deals supporting the momentum.

We expect the pace of this progress to accelerate towards 2H25 as regulatory ease alone will be insufficient to bring back the momentum seen in 2022-23; projects will also need to navigate other hurdles, such as financing, legal, environmental and shipping/vessel requirements. Furthermore, Trump’s rollout to boost LNG exports will not immediately come into effect as authorisation/sanctioning of new policies will require some time, especially with rising global competition and other scrutiny. Meanwhile, Trump’s efforts to end the current pause will be challenged by Biden’s rush to approve its environmental study on LNG.

An imposition of new tariffs on China could reignite the 2018 trade war between these two major LNG players. In 2018, China imposed a 10% tariff on US LNG, taking it higher to 25% in 2019 and thereby leading to a plunge in US LNG shipments to China. However, in 2021, China waived the imposed tariff, and US LNG made a huge comeback to the country, with China starting to sign long-term supply deals with the US.

As speculation about a second trade war grows amid Trump’s re-election, we expect the possibility of China retaliating against any tariff by restricting its LNG imports from the US, which will likely bring about changes in trade patterns. Under such circumstances, China could increase its LNG reliance from other destinations. With China’s LNG demand expected to grow further, compromised US cargoes can be substituted from Qatar, Russia and Australia, curtailing the shipping demand since voyages will be cut short as the US-China trade plummets. Thus, any trade war will dent LNG shipping.

We keep a cautious outlook for LNG trade getting impacted by the tariffs, especially with the shifting trade patterns in the last few years. The US became the largest LNG exporter, and China reinstated itself as the biggest buyer in 2023. LNG has become one of the most revenue-generating sectors for the US in the last couple of years. As the global LNG trade thrives, the countries will likely take a more cohesive approach, especially when competition intensifies further among the sellers, with massive liquefaction capacity building up and flooding the market by the end of this decade.

The US will reign supreme, becoming the largest LNG seller, needing to secure buyers. Meanwhile, Asia will remain the hub for LNG demand, registering a significant growth, especially in countries such as China and India, which are expected to increase their LNG demand manifold in the next five years.

According to Drewry’s Contracts database, China has secured considerable contractual capacity with the US since 2021, whereby supplies extend until 2040. As the largest buyer in the LNG market, it is unlikely that the US would want to hamper its future supplies, especially at a time when competition among LNG players is intensifying.

Source:Drewry Maritime Research

Based on the supply deals signed between the US and China, it is likely that China will source a large amount of US LNG in the next 20 years, which will solidify the LNG trade between the two.China has signed 26 mtpa of new contracted supply in the last three years (2021-24). More is likely to follow as projects in the US receive regulatory approvals following Trump’s suspension of the current pause. Most uncontracted volumes in the US will find buyers, mainly in Asia, strengthening the US-Asia trade in the long run, while Europe will remain cautious about signing any long-term deals.

In a nutshell, Trump’s actions to lift the current pause are unlikely to bring new momentum immediately into the market as the pace of LNG FID approvals or supply deals will gradually increase. We could see considerable momentum towards mid-2025 after Trump will have likely lifted the barriers. However, regulatory barriers will not necessitate LNG development; other nuances related to financing, contractual activities, LNG carrier requirements and international trade policies will also dictate the progress of the LNG market.

If there are any tariffs or trade wars, trade patterns could change in the future. Any trade war between the two major players—the US and China—could negatively impact LNG shipping. On the other hand, the US could also impose stringent sanctions on Russia, which would hamper the latter’s current LNG flows to Europe, further enhancing the role of US LNG in Europe.
