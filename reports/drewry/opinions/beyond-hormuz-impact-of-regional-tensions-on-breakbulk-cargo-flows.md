# Beyond Hormuz: Impact of regional tensions on breakbulk cargo flows

*16 Jun 2026*

The security incidents near the Strait of Hormuz have been impacting breakbulk and project cargo logistics in the Middle East over the past four months. Although a framework agreement between the US and Iran to halt hostilities and reopen the strait was announced on 15 June 2026, operational conditions remain uncertain and fragile. Recent ceasefire violations and intermittent military exchanges have limited the recovery of vessel traffic, keeping risk management, routing strategies and contingency planning at the forefront of breakbulk shipping.

While cargo is still being traded, more of it is unloaded at alternative ports instead of main Gulf hubs like Jebel Ali, Port Khalifa, Dammam and Jubail. This change is breaking up supply chains and leading to higher costs, less capacity and more operational challenges, which will likely continue into 2026.

Project Carriers (multi-purpose vessels with at least 55 t single crane capacity) make up about 8–9% of global vessel traffic inside the Strait of Hormuz and are especially at risk from disruptions because the trade relies on specialised ships and complex logistics. Since the conflict started in late February, which led to a near-total cessation of commercial traffic over the past four months and only a very gradual and uncertain resumption in June, Omani ports have taken on some UAE-bound cargo. At the same time, more cargo destined for Saudi Arabia has passed through the Kingdom’s Red Sea ports, with very limited and highly controlled vessel movements through the strait, subject to security conditions and clearance protocols.

General Cargo, by comparison, makes up only about 1–2% of vessel traffic through Hormuz ports, so the related global trade is less affected by disruptions in the Middle East. It can also be redirected more easily since the trade is flexible in terms of cargo, route and vessel type, helping reduce the overall impact on regional supply chains.

Project Carrier and General Cargo operators are now choosing ports that seem safer or easier to reach during disruptions. Sohar and Duqm in Oman, along with some Red Sea ports in Saudi Arabia, have become important hubs. These ports allow cargo to be unloaded into free zones for trucking to its final destination. But the sudden increase in freight has put pressure on these facilities, causing congestion, longer wait times and storage problems. Even with the announcement of a reopening framework, cargo owners remain cautious, continuing to favour established alternative routes until consistent safe passage is restored.

Inland logistics are changing as cargo re-routing becomes necessary despite tentative diplomatic progress.  Khorfakkan, a key container port near the Strait of Hormuz, could serve as an alternative gateway, but its capacity for breakbulk cargo is limited by heavy container traffic and out-of-gauge cargoes are not accepted at the moment. Long truck lines also slow things down. As a result, breakbulk cargo that used to head straight to UAE ports is now often sent through Omani ports, and shipments to Saudi Arabia are being redirected to Red Sea ports such as Jeddah and Yanbu.

These changes have revealed problems in the regional trucking network. Insufficient trucks, border delays and changing customs rules all make trade less efficient. Trucking costs have multiplied, mainly because of longer routes, higher fuel prices and not enough trucks for special cargo. With cargo owners focusing more on resilience than efficiency, shipments are being kept at intermediate hubs until the situation improves. For large project cargo, this increases the risk of storage damage, contract delays and higher project costs.

Despite a June 2026 framework agreement aimed at reopening the Strait of Hormuz, the situation is unlikely to normalise quickly. Persistent security concerns, incomplete demining and fragile ceasefire conditions mean that the disruption is expected to continue shaping breakbulk shipping routes in the near-to-medium term. Supply chains are increasingly regionalised, employing multimodal transport solutions and using a broader array of ports. The anticipated full operation of GCC rail networks by 2030 is expected to alleviate bottlenecks and enhance supply chain resilience. Additionally, bunker costs are projected to remain elevated through 2026. Most contracts now incorporate an 'uncertainty premium' to address fluctuations in fuel prices and geopolitical risks. With rising trucking and port handling expenses, overall logistics costs are reaching unprecedented levels.

In short, security risks at Hormuz are not stopping trade, but they are changing how breakbulk cargo moves. Costs and transit times are going up, and the industry will have to deal with a more uncertain and fragmented logistics environment through 2026, despite the expected start of partial normalisation later in the year.
