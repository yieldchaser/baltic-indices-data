# Shippers respond to an erratic airfreight market

*27 Aug 2025*

In our latest Logistics Executive Briefing, Tom Crabtree, leading airfreight analyst at Drewry, answers questions on some of the big developments and trends in the global airfreight market and how shippers are responding.

Q.1 2024 was a good year for air freight traffic growth. What trends in growth have you seen this year, in the context of tariff changes in the US?

After growing 11.4% in 2024, world airfreight traffic in 2025 has vacillated wildly from month-to-month between slight contraction or no growth to mid-single percentage growth year-over-year. World airfreight traffic, as measured in cargo tonne-kilometres (CTK), barely grew, expanding 0.8% year-over-year (YoY) in June 2025.  preliminary reports indicate that world airfreight traffic growth rebounded between 5 and 6% YoY in July 2025 – see chart.

E-commerce and sea-air traffic moving via South Asia and Middle East transit points were the biggest drivers of air cargo throughout 2024.  Since January 2025, however, a pall of uncertainty and volatility has settled over freight markets.  Due to decisions of the US presidential administration, US-centric containership and air cargo traffic levels have been halting as shippers struggle to make sense of changing regulations and/or beat tariff deadlines that are often re-set and/or postponed. As a result, many shippers that rushed to ’front load’ some shipments by airfreight one month, found themselves having to reverse course the next.

Q.2 Looking at the huge Far East-North America international air cargo market, what do the latest traffic volume numbers show?

Far East-North America, the biggest international air cargo market, contracted 4.8% YoY, a deceleration in large part due to falling demand for charters of large widebody freighters on the eastbound direction of that air trade lane.

Q.3 Traffic growth numbers in June were much higher on some non-US routes. What does this tell us?

Evidence that shippers and airlines are re-routing air trade away from direct routes to the United States can be found in the strongest year-to-date growth rates by airfreight trade lane.  Europe-Far East, Europe-North America and intra-Far East grew 8.9%, 8.4% and 7.2% respectively in the first six months of 2025 compared to the same period of 2024.  Middle East carriers were adversely affected by the airspace closures that were a result of the Israel/US-Iran airstrike exchanges that month. The impact of restrictions on China-originating air exports, particularly due to the collapse of low-value e-commerce shipments (under 'de minimis' rules) to the US that began in early May, became clearer in June.  Relatively stronger growth on Europe-Far East and Europe-North America trade lanes was very likely fuelled by 're-routing traffic' as shippers struggled to cope with ever-changing US trade policies, as well as 'front-loading' from Europe and a pivot of Chinese exporters to European markets.

Since 4Q 2023, approximately 90% of westbound containership sailings have re-routed around the Cape of Good Hope, adding ~10 days sailing time to Asia-to-Europe services, and forcing some shippers to use "sea-air" services primarily via Middle East airports to move freight from the Far East to Europe.  As a result, Europe-Middle East CTKs grew 26% in 2024, but that air trade lane fell 4.5% YoY in June (and -6.7% year-to-date through June 2025), as shippers have found lower-cost routings to avoid usage of airfreight on this region-to-region airfreight flow.

World airfreight capacity, as measured in available cargo tonne-kilometres (ACTK), expanded roughly double the rate of traffic, growing 1.7% YoY, with international capacity expanding faster at 2.8% YoY during June.

The North American airline capacity contraction in June (down 5.15 YoY) was sharper than the 3.5% fall witnessed in May.  On international airfreight trade lanes, passenger lower hold (“belly”) capacity grew 4.8%, whereas freighter capacity grew only 0.4% YoY.

Q.4 What factors are driving airfreight rates down?

Added passenger belly and freighter capacity, uncertain US trade policy, and falling jet fuel prices may have all acted in concert to exert downward pressure on airfreight yields in 2Q and early 3Q 2025.  Boeing and Airbus combined delivered 115 new passenger widebody aircraft and factory-built freighters year-to-date through July 2025, up 27 units over the same timeframe of 2024.  Evidence of pressure on head-haul rates ex-Asia to US gateways can be seen in Hong Kong-to-Los Angeles and Seoul-to-New York rates, but it is particularly visible in Shanghai-to-Chicago rates over the one-year period of July 2024 to July 2025.
