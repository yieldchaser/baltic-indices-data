# Declining utilisation in the dry bulk market weakens rates

*09 Nov 2023*

A significantly low utilisation of the fleet in 2023, aided by reduced waiting time, led to an increased supply stretch available for employment. As a result, freight rates have been substantially down this year despite a surge in demand.

The dry bulk trade is likely to record historically high volumes and increase by 4.5% in 2023 on account of buoyant coal trade and a resilient grain market.

China’s thermal coal imports surged by a phenomenal 75% YTD (end October), making coal inventories 14% higher on average. This rise is not just due to a favourable base of 2022, as imports are also substantially higher than the record levels seen in 2021. Moreover, coal imports will remain robust as hydropower generation remains historically low.

Similarly, global iron ore trade also improved by 1.5% even though most economies (except China) have reduced their imports. China’s iron ore imports surged by 5% while its inventories also dropped substantially, creating scope for higher imports to rebuild the inventories.

Meanwhile, massive grain exports from Russia and Brazil counterbalanced lower exports from Ukraine, Argentina, the EU and the US.

The fleet growth is likely to be as low as 2.6% in 2023. Meanwhile, effective supply (the supply adjusted with operating inefficiencies) is projected to rise by 3.4%, mostly due to reduced inactivity.

Despite the higher demand growth as compared to supply, rates weakened in 2023 as the global waiting time has reduced considerably, making more vessels available for employment.

There was massive congestion at several major ports in Europe last year due to increasing panic to source coal amid the Russia-Ukraine war and high gas prices. For instance, the waiting time at the Port of Rotterdam touched 60 days, tightening vessel supply, which boosted rates in the Atlantic.

Source:Drewry AIS

However, utilisation is likely to improve in 2024 as vessel supply stretch will decline. The average global congestion has already eased in 2023 and a further substantial contraction in congestion is unlikely from here. As the demand is likely to expand along with a cap on the increase in supply stretch, the utilisation of the fleet will go up, supporting freight rates in 2024.
