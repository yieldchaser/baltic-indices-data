# Licence to stall: US red tape threatens China’s ethane imports

*10 Jun 2025*

A new US Commerce Department directive requiring licenses for ethane exports to China has created massive uncertainty for VLECs and ethane trade, adding another layer of volatility to the ongoing US-China tariff standoff. US-China accounts for 55% of the global ethane trade, and the directive threatens the growth of the Chinese petchem sector.

Immediate operational impact:The new licensing regime introduced by the US Department of Commerce’s Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) mandates Validated End-User (VEU) licenses for ethane exports to China. The revocation of previously issued licences to certain suppliers has created further operational uncertainty. This move has already begun to disrupt vessel scheduling and berthing operations at key Gulf Coast terminals.

As a result, two VLECs—Pacific INEOS GrenadierandSTL Qianjiang—remain moored at Gulf Coast terminals, unable to load or depart.STL Qianjiang, typically committed to Satellite Chemical, has been loitering at Nederland since 26 May. Meanwhile, Wanhua Chemical’sGas Changjiangmay be rerouted, with its final destination unclear.

Meanwhile, major exporters such as Enterprise Products Partners and Energy Transfer are re-evaluating their laycan schedules after receiving a 'notice of intent to deny' regarding their emergency licence applications for shipments to China.

Terminal congestion, cargo diversions and the search for alternatives:The unpredictability created by this move has already resulted in vessel bunching and prolonged turnaround times at Nederland and Morgan’s Point terminal. With China accounting for over 55% of US seaborne ethane liftings in 2024, finding alternatives will be a challenging task.

While US ethane has alternative markets in Europe, India and Southeast Asia, only India—besides China— has VLEC-compatible berths, limiting re-routing options. However, India's ethane crackers can absorb only 2 mtpa ethane, translating to a throughput requirement of roughly four VLEC discharges per month. Consequently, India lacks the absorptive capacity to accommodate the full displaced volumes of US ethane cargoes originally destined for China.

These conditions are worsening US-China ethane trade flows, which had only recently stabilised following China’s decision to exempt US ethane from the previously imposed 125% retaliatory tariff.

“In response to US tariffs on Chinese goods, China imposed a 125% retaliatory tariff on US exports, which it decided to invoke, recognising the strategic importance of uninterrupted feedstock supply for its ethane-fed crackers.”

While the current mandate is anticipated to cause only short-term disruptions to ethane shipping, such interruptions can have long-term implications—particularly on the development trajectory of related infrastructure that is key to growing ethane trade.

Although the licensing mandate is expected to cause only short-term disruptions for ethane shipments, its ripple effects can have long-lasting implications on related infrastructure development. On China’s side, delayed approvals risk lowering ethane cracker utilisation rates, triggering shutdowns or deferring the startup of new crackers. On the US side, reduced terminal utilisation could push back the commissioning of new ethane export terminals, constraining ethane trade. Even with on-time startups, flexible terminals may favour propane exports. Additionally, heightened regulatory uncertainty can weigh on investor sentiments, prompting a slowdown in new ordering for VLECs in the near term.
