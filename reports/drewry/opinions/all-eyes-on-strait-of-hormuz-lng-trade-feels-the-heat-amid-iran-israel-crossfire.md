# All eyes on Strait of Hormuz: LNG trade feels the heat amid Iran-Israel crossfire

*18 Jun 2025*

The escalating Israel-Iran conflict has once again threatened energy shipping via the Strait of Hormuz, a crucial passage for LNG accounting for 21% of global supply, with over 1,000 loaded LNGCs from Qatar (Ras Laffan) and the UAE (Das Island) passing through the passage annually. Drewry expects a complete closure of the Strait of Hormuz to be improbable, especially for an extended period. However, as Iran contemplates and threatens to close the strait, we expect LNG prices to rally following potential supply disruptions and rates to gain ground amid trade alterations. Meanwhile, ship operators will exercise caution in the near term as tensions are likely to escalate and persist around the region.

Although short-term adjustments due to a partial closure could support rates by vessels avoiding the Middle East and repositioning, an extended blockage could choke the LNG trade, bringing dire consequences to LNG shipping.

Israel’s attack on Iran and the latter’s retaliation have spooked the Middle East and raised concerns about the LNG trade through the Strait of Hormuz. The passage is critical for LNG trade as it accounts for 21% of the global LNG supply, supporting exports from Qatar and the UAE (excluding Oman).

We expect any blockade to hamper the LNG market, affecting exports from Qatar and the UAE. The extent of the supply shortage will depend on the duration of the closure. According to Drewry AIS, Qatar exports about 0.2 million tonnes of LNG per day, marking 10-12 LNGC loadings daily, while the UAE loads one or two LNGCs a day.

Not smooth-sailing:As Asian buyers will be more exposed to disruptions in the Middle East than European buyers, we expect Asian Spot to rally amid a supply shortage. Although higher Asian LNG prices will incentivise exporters to ship their cargo to the East, the increased prices will dissuade price-sensitive buyers, especially from South and Southeast Asia.

Asia’s hunt for alternatives will challenge Europe’s comfortable position:China’s receded competition for US LNG, the country’s pivot to the Middle East and Russia, and Asia’s subdued demand comforted Europe’s position so far. However, supply disruptions in the Middle East will prompt Asian buyers to eye other markets, which will increase the competition for Europe. As a result, TTF will spike in tandem with Asian Spot, enabling the region to remain an ‘attractive destination’.

TTF futures have rallied (up 5% on 17 June, reaching the highest level in the past 10 weeks), following growing hostilities in the Middle East. This signals the fragility of market sentiments and Europe’s high sensitivity to supply disruptions.

Middle Eastern supply essential for summer demand:Countries such as Egypt and Iraq are also looking to source more LNG this summer, primarily from the Middle East. In case of a tight supply, these countries will have to alter their primary supply sources, spurring competition for ‘summer cargoes’. Moreover, regional buyers such as Kuwait and Bahrain will become highly reliant on internal gas supplies as importing from other destinations will become impossible.

However, the substitution will not be manageable for long. Impending summer demand and looming supply shortage will create a massive supply-demand imbalance, boosting prices. Under such circumstances, the market will turn into a desert, leaving many buyers without LNG.

China’s increasing reliance on Qatar’s LNG could be challenged amid supply disruption. The extended tensions in the Middle East could make China’s stance against US LNG feeble.  While China’s total LNG imports have been down 20% YoY in 2025 YTD, the country’s trade with Qatar has remained stable compared to that of its counterparts.

The trade war with the US has drastically reduced LNG shipments. We expect short-term supply disruption in the Middle East to be inadequate to push China back to the US, as China can source from other countries such as Russia and Australia. However, if the demand surges amid the harsh summer, China will be compelled to turn to US LNG. The revival of US-China trade could bring new momentum for LNG shipping, which has been absent since March amid the tariff war.

Short-lived gains are likely amid partial closure of the Strait of Hormuz, but can be a spoilsport if extended for longer

We believe the partial/short-term closure of the strait could support LNG shipping as buyers will import from other destinations, entailing longer voyages and higher tonne miles, supporting LNG shipping rates (which remained weak in 1H25 (down 65% YoY). Rates could surge mainly due to higher vessel absorption with LNGCs diverting from the Middle East to the US and US shipments taking the COGH over the Panama and Suez Canals, boosting tonne-mile demand.

Meanwhile, shipowners will steer clear of shipping cargoes in a war zone and will search for new employment. If the closure extends longer, a supply shortage will impair LNG shipping, with several vessels idling, inevitably denting LNG rates. The fundamental dynamics will also be impacted as fleet growth is already outpacing liquefaction growth in 2025 and 2026, with 90 and 95 LNGC deliveries expected, respectively.

Moreover, soaring LNG prices will dissuade price-sensitive buyers, and with demand plunging, it will dent the trade. Thus, an unprecedented supply-demand imbalance will lead to a rate crash in LNG shipping.
