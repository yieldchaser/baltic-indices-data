# The next Dangote? Kenya's proposed refinery could transform East Africa

*03 Aug 2026*

The successful commissioning of the 650,000 bpd Dangote Refinery transformed Nigeria from a structural importer of refined products into a growing regional supplier. A similar shift could now be on the horizon for East Africa with Nigerian billionaire Aliko Dangote proposing a 700,000 bpd refinery on Kenya’s Lamu Island with a similar aim, which will transform energy import dynamics in East Africa. If realised, the project would fundamentally alter the region’s refined product trade flows, reducing import dependence and reshaping demand for MR product tankers serving East Africa.

Unlike West Africa, which is gradually improving its domestic refining capacity, East Africa continues to rely almost entirely on imported refined products due to the absence of large-scale commercial refineries. Rising fuel demand, supported by population growth, urbanisation and industrialisation, has driven imports steadily higher over the past decade. The Middle East and India dominate these supply flows, with most cargoes transported on MR product tankers.

Transport fuels account for the majority of regional demand. Gasoline and diesel together represent around 66% of East Africa's seaborne refined product imports, underlining the region's dependence on road transport fuels. Other refined products, including fuel oil, naphtha and LPG, account for 28%, while kerosene and jet fuel contribute the remaining 6%.

The proposed 700,000 bpd refinery in Kenya has the potential to transform East Africa's refined products market in much the same way that the Dangote Refinery reshaped West Africa. When the proposal is approved (likely by end-2026), construction is expected to take about 2.5 years. However, large-scale projects in Africa have historically experienced delays, as evidenced by the Dangote Refinery, which faced multiple setbacks due to financing constraints and other challenges. Assuming the project progresses on schedule, commercial operations are expected to commence in late 2029.

The refinery is expected to become the primary supply hub for Kenya, Uganda, South Sudan, Rwanda, Burundi and the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), significantly reducing the region's reliance on long-haul imports from the Middle East and India.

While the 700,000 bpd refining capacity has been officially announced, the detailed product yield (such as diesel, gasoline, jet fuel) has not yet been disclosed. However, since the project is being developed by Dangote Industries and is expected to closely mirror the design and configuration of the 650,000 bpd Dangote Refinery in Lagos, it is reasonable to use Dangote's publicly available product slate as a proxy for estimating future output.

Based on this assumption, the projected post-commissioning product balance suggests that East Africa would move from being a structurally import-dependent region to one with substantial domestic refining capacity and export potential. This shift would significantly reduce gasoline and diesel imports from the Middle East and India, increase intra-East African product flows and structurally lower demand for long-haul MR tankers.

The largest impact of the proposed refinery in Kenya would be felt on trades from the Middle East and India to East Africa, which are currently dominated by MR product tankers.

Once the refinery reaches full operating capacity, the reduction in long-haul imports could remove demand equivalent to more than 55 MR tanker voyages annually. While East Africa accounts for around 3% of global seaborne refined products trade, its importance is greater for gasoline, which represents approximately 5% of global seaborne gasoline imports. Consequently, gasoline trades are expected to experience the largest displacement.

Some of the lost tonne-mile demand would be offset by increased coastal and intra-African product movements as Kenyan exports are redistributed across neighbouring markets. Nevertheless, shorter regional voyages cannot fully compensate for the loss of long-haul imports from the Middle East and India, leaving the overall impact negative for MR tanker demand.

Despite its strategic importance, the project faces significant execution risks. Environmental groups and local activists have already raised concerns over the proposed development, adding to the regulatory and financing challenges typically associated with large infrastructure projects in Africa.

While the refinery has the potential to redefine East Africa's energy supply chain, its impact on tanker markets will ultimately depend on whether the project is delivered on schedule and reaches its planned operating capacity.
