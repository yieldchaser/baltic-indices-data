# Proposed USTR fees to take MPV freight costs sky high

*21 Mar 2025*

About 98% Project Carrier (including Deck Carriers) calls at US ports in the last 12 months would have been impacted by the proposed entrance fees from the USTR, as 76% of these calls are made by China-built ships, while 22% are by non-China-built ships which also face fines since their operators have China-built ships in their fleet. In the General Cargo category, 56% port calls would have been subjected to these fees, since 34% of the vessels are China-built.

In the last 12 months, 60% of calls at US ports by MPVs were made by Project Carriers, while General Cargo ships accounted for 40%. This highlights a greater impact on Project Carriers, with 76% of their calls involving China-built vessels while only 22% were made by non-China-built ships, which still had to pay entrance fees due to USTR regulations regarding operators’ fleet and orderbook.

Among the General Cargo fleet, 34% are China-built while 22% comprise vessels that would be impacted as their operators have China-built vessels in their fleet. In such a case, only 44% would remain unaffected by the proposed US fines. This is mainly because the General Cargo fleet consists of older ships, most of which were built outside China, that trade primarily on US-South America and US-Europe routes. Interestingly, a large portion of the non-China-built ships are small, a lot of the larger vessel will be Chinese, so this will make the percentages more dramatic. In contrast, Project Cargo includes vessels that are less than 15-20 years old, of which many are China-built and feature significant trade between the US and China, as well as the US and Europe.

Currently, 27% of the General Cargo vessels face fines of $1.5 million, while 54% are exempt from any fines when entering US ports. This positions General Cargo ships among the least affected categories by the newly proposed regulations compared to other sectors. The explanation is simple: As 41% of General Cargo ships are over 20 years of age, very few were built in China, since the Chinese shipbuilding industry became a significant player in the past two decades.

USTR-proposed fee will impact Project Cargo ships more than their General Cargo counterparts, as only 15% of the former ships are exempt from fines. This limitation leaves shippers with a very small number of vessels that are exempt from penalties. Also, most of the US-flagged vessels used by the shippers in the US trade are built in China, raising questions about how regulations will apply and whether these vessels will receive exemptions. If exemptions are granted, it could lead to some reflagging; however, this would contradict the goal of encouraging US-built ships. Consequently, the market will be divided into two tiers: China-built ships in addition to a limited number of ships that are neither China-built nor operated by Chinese carriers. As a result, the latter operators are likely to gain market share and higher profitability transporting goods to the US ports.

The focus of US trade may shift toward the leading operators of Project Carriers who own only non-China-built ships in their fleets as these 180 vessels will be exempt from entrance fees at US ports (note Russian assets unlikely to serve the US trade given the geopolitical situation). However, if demand remains high and shippers need the ships, which is most likely, other leading operators with a mix of China and non-China-built ships may also be approached for the availability of non-China-built ships. In such cases, fines could range from $0.5 million to $1 million.

The below analysis focuses on the impact of the US entrance fees on three types of ships voyaging from Hong Kong to Los Angeles. The cost per tonne of cargo may increase significantly, rising from 200% to 300%. Smaller ships will struggle to absorb these fees, while larger ships with a deadweight tonnage of 40,000 or more may manage better due to economies of scale. However, this price increase is not sustainable in a free market, and charterers are likely to look for alternatives to avoid these fines.

While it is too early to discuss the specific impacts in detail, we believe implementing heavy fines in a free market is unfeasible. From the US perspective, there is a concerted effort to re-establish the nation as a key player in the shipbuilding industry, but imposing substantial fines too quickly could lead to significant disruptions in the shipping sector, as these fines might sometimes exceed the total costs of a shipping voyage. Moving forward this cost will most likely be pushed onto the end user, in the form of higher freight rates. Drewry believes the proposed USTR fees could lead to major supply chain disruptions across various industries reliant on the seaborne shipment of large and heavy project cargoes.
