# Can 'green ammonia' be the future marine fuel?

*09 Jun 2021*

The IMO’s emissions reduction targets for 2030 and 2050 have set the stage for all marine fuel candidates to bag the title of ‘carbon-efficient fuel’. LNG and LPG are the front-runners, thanks to their well-established markets and low sulphur content. However, with the evolution of decarbonisation goals, their position is challenged by new greener fuels such as hydrogen and green ammonia.

Ammonia and hydrogen have zero-carbon content, whereas carbon emissions are significantly higher in competing fuels, such as heavy fuel oil (HFO), methanol, LPG and LNG. Ammonia and LPG have higher boiling points compared to other fuels, which enables them to be transported under low refrigeration. For instance, LPG is transported at -42 °C while ammonia is transported at -33°C. On the contrary, LNG and hydrogen require a temperature of -162°C and -253°C for transportation, requiring specialised vessels with higher capex and operating costs.

Over the years, there has been growing interest in green ammonia as marine fuel because of its zero-carbon and sulphur content. However, it must pass prerequisite factors to be preferred over hydrogen and LNG.

The first factor is affordability. By a simple price comparison, we can see how green ammonia can be a tough competitor to LPG, LNG and VLSFO in the futures, provided its production ramps up. It should be said that a perfect marine fuel should have set pricing mechanisms with low volatility to maintain vessel earnings.

The second requirement is the commodity’s supply security. Shipowners require a secure and uninterrupted supply of marine fuel, which influences its wider adoption. Ample production and robust distribution infrastructure are crucial to make a case for ammonia as a bunker fuel. In this respect its production is projected to reach 150 million tonnes by 2050.

We believe that the rise in production capacity of green ammonia will be insufficient, which may force shipowners to compete with other industries such as fertilizer to source the commodity. Several green ammonia projects have been announced (likely to come online between 2025 and 2040) in renewable resource-rich countries such as Australia, Germany, Oman, Saudi Arabia and the US. However, widespread adoption of green ammonia as marine fuel would require planned production capacity to ramp up at a much larger scale.

Furthermore, anhydrous ammonia is widely traded through 300 well-established ports around the globe. Of them, 120 ports are equipped with ammonia terminals with necessary storage facilities to form a foundation for it to be used as marine fuel.

At present, no vessel is equipped to use ammonia as a marine fuel. However, a handful of projects are in their initial stages to develop ammonia-fuelled marine engine propulsion.

The development of green ammonia as marine fuel has its own set of challenges. Ammonia offers less energy density, and ammonia-fuelled vessels require more storage volumes onboard. Furthermore, ammonia is a toxic commodity that is corrosive and can be lethal for crew members when inhaled even for a short duration.

That said, green ammonia is rapidly attracting the attention of shipping companies. While it readily meets the carbon neutrality criteria at a competitive cost, along with easy transportation, there are concerns regarding its secure supply and hazardous nature.

The safe handling of the fuel can be attained with some upgrades in the technical installations, for instance, through the installation of gas detection and automated safety protection equipment in ammonia-fuelled vessels. However, what may create a roadblock for green ammonia in becoming a future marine fuel is the lack of ample production.

While we expect green ammonia to be used as marine fuel due to its favourable environmental features, ramping up of its production may be difficult curbing its widespread use. However, we anticipate a wave of new orders for ammonia-fuelled vessels from 2026, with the first one scheduled to be delivered by 2024.Viking Energy(built-2003, 25,735 cbm) will be the first ammonia-fuelled capable vessel, currently being retrofitted at Sustainable Energy Norwegian Catapult Centre.
