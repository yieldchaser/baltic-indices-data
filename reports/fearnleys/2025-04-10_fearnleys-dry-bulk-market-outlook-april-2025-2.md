### SUMMARY AND OUTLOOK 

Adding Trump's trade war to other factors, this year looks more bearish than before. Potentially much more bearish. 

At the time of writing this, there is a de facto trade embargo between the two most important economies in the world. 
The countries that had not been so rude as to retaliate against Trump have now been "rewarded" with a less severe punishment, and invited to come and crawl before him over a 90-day period. The behavior is more like that of a feudal King than an American President. 
It is highly doubtful whether, over this 90-day period, there will be an orderly, fair, and intellectual negotiating process between the USA and the other trading partners. 
The premise Trump has put behind the negotiations is that all other countries are cheating against the USA. Baked into the initial tariffs he slapped on everybody was an assumption that apart from the actual tariffs, sinister currency manipulations, export subsidies, and other unfair practices have taken place over decades. No documentation was provided to prove that these manipulations were going on. All economists we have seen comment on it say that even if these manipulations were going on, it would be extremely hard, if not impossible, to quantify the effects of it. Further, Trump seems to want a trade surplus with every other country, which is impossible, and displays complete economic illiteracy. 
How will negotiations go with that considered?

What has been the real reason why the USA has had massive trade deficits for decades? The status of the US dollar as the global reserve currency. Since the US dollar became the global reserve currency, there has been strong demand to accumulate dollar reserves, more specifically, risk-free US government bonds. The US could thus finance its deficits by issuing currency, an advantage that already decades ago was dubbed by some an "exorbitant privilege" (exorbitant meaning unfairly large or disproportionate). 
This privilege was probably deserved, as it was the USA that, in large part, built the modern economic structures we have. This of course included the last decades push to lower global trade barriers, which have contributed to bringing billions of people out of poverty. 
The ability of the United States to fund deficits by issuing reserve currency is, in a sense, a financial imbalance, and this financial imbalance has probably to some degree contributed to a physical imbalance. There could indeed be relatively less manufacturing going on in the United States compared to what it would have been had the United States not had the reserve currency. 
However, putting up massive trade barriers as the solution to this imbalance is catastrophic considering that the world economy is entirely globalized. 
Under Trump's proposed policies, all Americans will get poorer either through higher costs of imported goods and a crashing stock market. Further, Trump will for sure not get the CAPEX investments he is seeking under the degree of uncertainty he is creating. A few days ago, US tariffs on imported goods from the EU was 30%, then a few days after, it was 10% - what will it be in 2 weeks? Nobody can plan long-term investments under such circumstances. 

Concerning dry bulk markets, cyclical indicators like energy costs, interest rates, and a Chinese stimulus will likely be pointing even more towards a bullish 2026 and 2027 than before. However, it is of the **utmost importance** that the trade wars end for that to happen. Why? Because pushing the same old stimulus buttons that one has become used to will have a different effect than before, should Trump's new trade regime be put into place permanently. If more money chases fewer goods, inflation will move up rapidly and put a quick end to an eventual upturn in demand, eventually leaving you worse off than before. 

Regarding estimates for earnings averages, we previously held the view that this year looked weaker than 2023. One could cling to the hope that the "direct effect" on dry bulk volumes is not too significant (USA exports made up 6% of the global total last year). But this is meaningless in a highly interconnected World economy. 

The outlook remains bearish until there is an end to the trade war. We hope for a swift resolution of ongoing events.

Through Q1, China's imported iron ore consumption was 4.7% higher than the same period in 2024. Notwithstanding positive year-on-year figures, average consumption for the first quarter was still lower than in the fourth quarter of last year. Q4 2024 consumption in turn was just slightly higher than in Q3, and the same level as in Q2. 
So, imported iron ore consumption in China has not increased for nearly a year, and inventories are higher than the same period last year. 

Trump's reciprocal tariffs will cause a drop in global steel demand, so the growth outlook for 2025 has worsened further. Next year, growth will improve considerably if there is an end to the trade war.

In our report last month, we wrote the following:

*Coal market fundamentals are weak. Import demand is significantly weaker in China and India, with reports of some companies in China set to halt imports this spring. India's demand is weighed down by a weakening macroeconomic backdrop and higher domestic production. 

India's import demand is not expected by Argus to recover before the second half of this year. China's record-high coal inventories need to be drawn down significantly before one can expect a stronger import market there. 
In the rest of the World, there is not much strength to trace either. Developed economies' imports will be low due to weak manufacturing growth and a shift toward greener types of energy.*

Coal prices keeps falling, and the outlook for demand has worsened due to Trump's reciprocal tariffs. Expectations for further price falls mean those who can will use inventories instead of new purchases. If prices fall enough, it will stimulate demand. Newcastle coal futures are slightly below 100 dollars per ton at the time of writing, so there is still much downside before the levels of 2015/16 or 2019/20 are reached...

## MACRO ECONOMIC OUTLOOK

Should the ongoing trade war continue or escalate, we are not sure whether any previously used market indicator can be trusted like before. For example, domestic fiscal stimulus and interest rate cuts might just lead to a short-lived upturn and outsized spike in inflation compared to the economic framework we are used to, as more money will change fewer goods. 

Top left chart:

China's total social financing growth seems to have turned a corner. The Chinese government will be incentivized to continue stimulus efforts given the current situation. However, as written initially in this report, massive trade barriers mean the usual stimulus measures will only lead to high inflation, thus the potential demand upturn will be highly short-lived. 

Top right chart:

The economic growth index was negative basis the released numbers for January and February. Given that the US consumer is very important for China's economy, the index will turn more deeply negative going forward. 

Middle left chart:

Comment from our previous report from early March:

*The USDCNY year-on-year change continues to predict China's dry bulk import growth with high accuracy. The USDCNY suggests China's import growth will be around 0% this year.*

The problem with currency indicators going forward is that Trump's policies could severely shake the foundations of the global monetary system. Counterparty risk is evident from the trading in US treasuries, as yields have risen since Liberation Day despite increasing expectations of Federal Reserve interest rate cuts. 

Middle right chart:

Global interest rate cuts suggest 1 year TC rates will be higher during the second half of 2026 than what it has been in Q1. That implies at least the mid-teens for Kamsarmaxes. 

Bottom left chart:

Given China's weak import growth, the global economic cycle is not suggested to bottom before Q1 2026. This was at least the indication before Trump started his trade wars. 

Bottom right chart:

Can currency indicators be trusted like before? What does global trade wars mean for the US dollar? Will a weak dollar signify that global liquidity conditions have improved, or does it reflect flight or diversification away from the dollar? As of now, the "old regime" still suggest tight global liquidity, which in turn is bearish for dry bulk markets.
