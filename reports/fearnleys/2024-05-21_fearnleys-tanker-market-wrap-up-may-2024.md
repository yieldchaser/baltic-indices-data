### Summary and outlook

Tanker market strength continues, with rates overall holding at quite firm levels going into what is normally a seasonally slower period. Apart from LR strength in the East, building on Red Sea evasion, rates across tanker segments are slightly more aligned than over the past couple of years. 

We believe the rest of the year and onwards will bring further normalisation, with the biggest vessels benefiting from a potentially stronger demand set-up and lower fleet growth than for smaller sizes. 

In the near term we expect rather flattish rate development going into what is normally a seasonally weaker period. Oil production increases and low fleet growth may offset some of the 'normal' seasonally softer factors through the summer, although some softer patches cannot be ruled out. 


![Rates by segment](https://pbrkapp.blob.core.windows.net/report/c9b6c4da-3cc9-413e-8239-e2a5c3ebae6b/rates.png)
*Figure: Rates by segment*

Rates strengthened again in May. For VLCCs this was driven by a tick up in cargo counts (likely due to timing) and correspondingly shorter position lists. The MEG-Far East average VLCC TCE in May to date has been $53k/day, up from $42k/d in April and slightly above our $50k/d bull case forecast for May. With a softening trend over the last week the full month average may end up closer to our forecast. 

Per our bull case forecast curve we expect rates to remain at strong levels through the next month or two before potentially dipping into what is normally the weakest months of the year. However, risk could be on the upside toward the end of the summer when seasonal demand in oil producing countries tails off and exports may  increase.

### Tanker trade flow

Overall tanker volumes have developed sideways to slightly lower lately, in line with a normal seasonal trend. Although crude oil production is expected to rise going forward, higher domestic consumption in key production areas through the summer means volumes are likely to trend sideways in coming months. 

The exception to this may be if the OPEC+ decides to reverse some cuts into the second half of the year. The broad consensus expectation, however, is that the group will stay put. Some signs in the physical market of sufficient supply supports this, and while energy agencies forecast a shortage of crude oil in the second half of this year next year looks more balanced.

*Figure: Seaborne crude oil volumes*


![Seaborne oil product volumes](https://pbrkapp.blob.core.windows.net/report/c9b6c4da-3cc9-413e-8239-e2a5c3ebae6b/volp.png)
*Figure: Seaborne oil product volumes*

Tonne-miles, meanwhile, have risen YTD. Through the first four months, preliminary data shows 3% higher tonne-miles vs. the same period last year. This increase is largely driven by Red Sea evasion as overall tanker volumes over the same period were 1% lower y/y. 

In the second half of the year, after peak summer oil demand ebbs, seaborne volumes are again slated to rise, and as West-East trade is by then likely to be the primary driver, tonne-mile demand should rise further. This is mostly expected to boost the larger tanker segments. 

For now, however, the West-East arbitrage has narrowed despite the signs of a weaker physical oil market in the Atlantic basin. The latter is something to keep an eye on as further weakness and a following re-opening of the West-East arbitrage to a greater extent could spur some life into the VLCC market..

Red Sea volumes remain significantly lower, and a larger portion of what used to go through has found its way around Africa. Through April, crude oil volumes transiting the Bab-el-Mandeb strait had declined by nearly 1.6 mbpd while products had declined by nearly 2.6 mbpd vs. the 2023 average. At the same time, crude oil passing the Cape of Good Hope had increased by 1 mbpd and products by 2 mbpd. Hence, still it is not a one-for-one shift, with some more oil trading locally, but the overall tonne-mile effect remains meaningful.

### Oil market balance

Volumes have largely moved sideways, in line with a seasonal pattern so far this year, but oil market balances point to upside going forward. Both oil demand and supply are expected to rise materially in coming months, and further next year. A tighter oil market going forward is priced in, with a backwardated oil price forward curve (although diesel is in contango..) and refinery margins which remain above historical averages (although have softened somewhat lately). 


![Brent 7-1 months forward spread vs. oil market balance](https://pbrkapp.blob.core.windows.net/report/c9b6c4da-3cc9-413e-8239-e2a5c3ebae6b/fwdbal.png)
*Figure: Brent 7-1 months forward spread vs. oil market balance*

In its most recent monthly report, the IEA's implied balance points to a slightly tighter market than previously expected through the remainder of the year. Global oil supply is expected to rise by 0.6, 1.0 and 0.3 mbpd Q/Q in Q2, Q3 and Q4, respectively. Most all of that is driven by non-OPEC+, but still there would be room for the OPEC+ to lift production in the second half. That does not seem to be the market's expectation, however. Given the prevailing market narrative, that would likely give quite a bit softer oil prices (which in itself should be able to stimulate some demand upside...), but a bigger problem, perhaps, for the OPEC+ is that next year's balances imply an oversupplied market - even if the group does not reverse cuts. 

Non-OPEC+ production is expected to increase by 1.4 mbpd y/y both this and next year. The U.S. is still expected to contribute the most, with 640 kbpd this year and 580 kbpd next year, although this is a lower market share than in previous years with Brazil, Canada and Guyana taking up a growing share. 


![Oil market balance](https://pbrkapp.blob.core.windows.net/report/c9b6c4da-3cc9-413e-8239-e2a5c3ebae6b/oilbal.png)
*Figure: Oil market balance*

OECD inventories declined to a 20-year low in March, contrary to preliminary data in last month’s report which pointed to a bigger build. Preliminary April data point to a build. With a growing surplus of crude oil in the Atlantic basin due to stagnant demand and growing supply, there could be room for further inventory builds going forward. However, economics currently do not support that, and hence there could rather be room for more West-East trade. 

Implied Chinese crude oil balances still point to steady crude oil inventory builds, although various satelite tracking services point to stock draws earlier this year. Some of the difference may be explained by building oil products inventories.

### Refining and products

Refinery margins have largely softened recently, indicating somewhat weaker underlying demand. Especially diesel/gasoil margins have come off, to something closer to historical averages, with the forward curve also flipping into contango. Earlier this year there was a bigger discrepancy between Western and Eastern refinery margins, but these have levelled slightly in recent weeks, which could give less East-West trade opportunities. 

Overall, as margins remain somewhat above the historical averages and new refineries come onstream, refinery runs are expected to reach new all-time-highs this year - 1 mbpd higher than last year. Next year too, runs are expected to rise, by 0.7 mbpd. 

While this bodes well for product tanker demand, one thing to consider is the impact of the start-up of the Dangote refinery in Nigeria and the elusive Dos Boccas in Mexico (when or if). Once fully operational, these are likely to alleviate some of the Atlantic basin gasoil/diesel shortages. 

In 2021 and 2022 Europe (ex. Turkey) imported about 650 kbpd of gasoil/diesel from Russia. In 2023 the replacement of Russian barrels came in the form of a 300 kbpd decline in overall imports (lower demand), and about 100 kbpd higher imports from each of the Middle East, Asia (mainly India) and North America. As the Dangote and Dos Bocas refineries combined are likely to produce more gasoil/diesel than Europe has recently imported from the Middle East and Asia, this leg of the last year's tonne-mile increase may at some point reverse.

### Tanker fleet development

The orderbook to fleet remains on the low side, although keeps creeping up. There is still a long way to go before the ratio hits figures which would historically be associated with (too) high fleet growth. 



![Orderbook to fleet](https://pbrkapp.blob.core.windows.net/report/c9b6c4da-3cc9-413e-8239-e2a5c3ebae6b/obfleet.png)
*Figure: Orderbook to fleet*

Regardless, fleet growth between 2024-26 will be low, and although there remains open capacity for 2027 deliveries at yards, this is not likely to become a very high fleet growth year either. 


![Historical deliveries and orderbook by segment](https://pbrkapp.blob.core.windows.net/report/c9b6c4da-3cc9-413e-8239-e2a5c3ebae6b/dely.png)
*Figure: Historical deliveries and orderbook by segment*


![Deliveries vs. phase-out potential](https://pbrkapp.blob.core.windows.net/report/c9b6c4da-3cc9-413e-8239-e2a5c3ebae6b/obout.png)
*Figure: Deliveries vs. phase-out potential*

The highest relative fleet growth in coming years is expected for the smaller segments - perhaps the flipside of relatively stronger earnings the past years. VLCC deliveries this and next year are few and far between, and although the delivery tallies rise in 2026 and 2027, these years may also be relatively low in a historical context. 

Ordering has picked up this year, although the pace has slowed somewhat most recently. That is, for confirmed orders at least, as there still remains a number of reported or rumoured orders which could lift the tally further in coming months. Still, that may be needed, considering the outlook for continued demand growth, limited deliveries in coming years and an already quite tight tanker market balance. 

Meanwhile, only three vessels have been scrapped year to date, one Suez, one Afra and one Handy, following just slightly more than a handful of vessels last year. As earnings remain strong, and the outlook is mainly a positive one, we do not expect to see a whole lot of scrapping, other than the odd vessel here or there, also going forward.
